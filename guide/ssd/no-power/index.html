⚡ SSD (SATA and NVMe) · 🪫 Won't power on / dead

SSD Dead or Won't Power On

A dead SSD may be fine, and the problem lies in the cable, port, adapter or power supply. If those check out, the controller (the chip that runs the SSD) or a power part on the SSD may have failed. SSDs have no moving parts, so there is nothing to listen for.

4/5 · High risk
Success odds: Low

Checking cables is safe, but repeatedly powering a drive with a short circuit can damage it more.

✋ Stop! Don't do this first

  • Do not keep plugging it in if you smell burning or see scorch marks.
  • Do not try to repair the circuit board yourself.
  • Do not keep turning it on and off again and again hoping it comes back.

Your steps

0 of 7 done
  1. Show me how
    1. Unplug the SSD and hold it in good light.
    2. Look at the green or black circuit board for black marks, cracks, missing parts or a burnt smell.
    3. Look at the gold connector edge or pins for bends or dirt.
    4. If you see any damage, stop here and go to the professional step.
  2. Show me how
    1. Swap the cable or adapter for a different one you know works.
    2. Try another USB port, ideally one directly on the computer, not a hub.
    3. Try a different computer.
    4. Windows: right-click the Start button and choose Disk Management. Mac: press Cmd+Space, type Disk Utility and press Enter, then click View > Show All Devices.
  3. Show me how
    1. M.2 SSD: move it to another M.2 slot, or put it in a USB-NVMe enclosure (NVMe is the type of fast M.2 SSD).
    2. SATA SSD: try a different power cable from the power supply, or use a USB-to-SATA adapter that has its own power.
    3. Try each way only once or twice. Do not keep re-plugging it.
    4. If nothing shows, move on to the wait step.
  4. Show me how
    1. Restart the computer and tap the setup key (usually F2, Del or F10) to reach the BIOS (the first screen before Windows or macOS starts). Stay on that screen.
    2. Leave the SSD powered for about 10 minutes.
    3. Touch it now and then. If it gets hot, unplug it at once.
    4. Shut down, then check whether it now shows up. Do not repeat this.
  5. Show me how
    1. You need a second drive with free space at least as big as the SSD. Plug it in and note its name.
    2. Windows: ddrescue runs from Linux, so follow the Live USB guide to start your PC from a Linux USB stick (a stick that starts a temporary Linux without touching your drive). Mac: press Cmd+Space, type Terminal, press Enter, then type brew install ddrescue (needs Homebrew from brew.sh).
    3. Find the SSD's name. Linux: type lsblk and match it by size (it looks like /dev/sdb). Mac: type diskutil list (it looks like /dev/disk4). Pick by size, never guess.
    4. Mac only: type diskutil unmountDisk /dev/disk4 (use your number) so the Mac lets go of it. Do not click Initialize or Erase if a window pops up.
    5. Type sudo ddrescue -d /dev/sdX /mnt/backup/.img /mnt/backup/map.log replacing sdX and the folder with yours. The first path is the SSD (the source), the second is where the copy goes. Swapping them erases the SSD.
    6. Wait until it says it is finished. Keep the map.log file: it lets you stop and resume. Work only on an image file from now on.

  6. Show me how
    1. Open TestDisk (a free tool that finds lost partitions, meaning the sections of a drive, and deleted files). Windows: unzip the download from cgsecurity.org and double-click testdisk_win.exe. Mac or Linux: in Terminal type sudo testdisk.
    2. If you made an image, open it by typing testdisk image.img (Windows: run this in Command Prompt from the TestDisk folder). Otherwise choose the drive from the list with the arrow keys and press Enter.
    3. Choose Create (log file), then pick the partition table type it suggests, usually Intel/PC or EFI GPT, and press Enter.
    4. Choose Analyse, then Quick Search, and copy any files you see to a different drive. If nothing shows, use PhotoRec on the image.
    5. Use the arrow keys and Enter to move around and q to go back. TestDisk only changes anything if you choose Write, so do not choose it yet.

  7. Show me how
    1. Decide how valuable the files are. If they cannot be replaced, do not experiment further.
    2. Search for data recovery labs near you and read their pricing page. Look for a free evaluation and a 'no data, no fee' promise.
    3. Ask them about your exact problem before you send anything. Do not pay a large fee up front.
    4. Pack the drive in an anti-static bag or bubble wrap and post it only after you agree on terms.

Questions people ask

How can I tell if my SSD is dead or just the cable?

Try a different cable, port and computer. If it fails everywhere, the drive itself is the problem.

Does a dead SSD mean my data is gone?

Not necessarily, but many SSDs scramble data and tie it to the controller, so recovery is harder than with hard drives.
